The Latency Budget Nobody Signed Off On

by A. Draft · unpublished · v0.3

DRAFT — not for publication

Every product team I have worked with has a performance budget. Almost none of them agreed to it. The budget is not the number in the tech spec; it is the sum of every small decision that shipped last quarter — a font here, a tag manager there, a chart library nobody quite owns. By the time anyone measures it, the budget has already been spent, and the conversation turns into an audit instead of a design discussion.

This is a draft of an argument I keep making in review meetings, written down so I can stop repeating it. Latency is a product decision: decide it deliberately, or it gets decided for you by whoever merged last.

The three numbers worth arguing about

Most dashboards report a dozen metrics and change no behaviour. Three are enough to run a team on, provided you argue about them in public:

  • Time to first byte — the honest measure of your backend and your CDN configuration, uncontaminated by anything the browser does.
  • Largest contentful paint — the closest proxy for "did the thing I came for appear," and the one product managers actually feel.
  • Interaction to next paint — the number that catches the pages that look finished and behave like wet cement.

Pick a target for each, write it in the same document as the feature requirements, and treat a regression as a bug rather than as tech debt. The point is not the specific thresholds; it is that a budget with no owner is a wish.

Where the budget actually goes

Ask a team where their page weight lives and you will usually hear "images." Images are the polite answer. In practice the expensive part is almost always execution, not transfer: a megabyte of JPEG costs you bandwidth once, while a megabyte of JavaScript costs you parse, compile, and a main thread that cannot respond to a tap.

The pattern repeats across every audit I have run. Third-party tags arrive as one line in a template and leave as four hundred milliseconds on a mid-range Android. Nobody added them maliciously; each one was locally reasonable. HTTP/2, standardised in 2015, was supposed to make bundling obsolete overnight, and a generation of build configs was rewritten on that promise — yet the waterfalls I look at today are still dominated by a handful of blocking requests that arrive too late to matter.

Measuring in the wild

Lab numbers are reproducible and field numbers are true. You need both, and teams that only have one of them tend to argue past each other for a quarter before noticing.

Making the budget stick

The mechanism matters more than the metric. A budget enforced by a check that runs on every pull request will outlive three reorgs; a budget that lives in a quarterly slide will not survive the quarter. Put the check where the code is, fail the build on a regression above the noise floor, and give people a documented way to say "yes, on purpose" — an escape hatch with a name attached is far better than one that gets added quietly.

A performance budget is not a diet. It is a currency: it lets you say yes to the expensive feature you actually want by saying no to the four you did not notice you were buying.

The teams that get this right do not talk about milliseconds much. They talk about what they are spending them on, and they can tell you who signed off.
